Begun as an argument about what Zimbabwean children are owed

Mazowe Heights was founded in 2023 by a group of eleven Zimbabwean educators, parents and alumni of the country's older schools who were tired of a single trade-off: send your child abroad for an international curriculum, or keep them home and accept fewer laboratories, fewer books and larger classes.

The land was acquired in 2024 — 140 hectares of the Mazowe Valley, an hour from Harare, with water rights and room to grow. Construction began in September 2025. The first classrooms were handed over in July 2026, the science block follows in October, and the fourth boarding house completes in November.

We open in January 2027 with 420 pupils and 46 teaching staff, and we will grow to 900 by 2032. We are deliberately starting smaller than we could. A founding cohort sets the culture of a school for a generation, and that is not something to rush.

A new school asks something unusual of the families who join it first: trust, before there is any record to trust.

I have spent twenty-two years in Zimbabwean and South African classrooms, and I have watched brilliant children be limited by things that had nothing to do with them — a laboratory with no reagents, a class of fifty-two, a curriculum chosen for them by circumstance. Mazowe Heights exists to remove those particular limits.

What we promise is specific. Classes capped at twenty-four. Every science practical actually performed. Two examination pathways resourced identically. A tutor who knows your child by name and reports to you in person three times a year. Published fees with no surprise levies. And a school that tells you when it gets something wrong.

What we ask in return is that you treat this as a partnership. Come to the parent evenings. Read the reports. Tell us when your child is struggling before we notice. The founding cohort will set the tone of this place for the next thirty years, and their parents will set it with them.

I look forward to meeting you at the gate in January.

Mazowe Heights is a registered Cambridge International School (centre approval granted 2026). Pupils sit Cambridge IGCSE at the end of Form 4 and Cambridge International AS and A Level in the Sixth Form, with examinations in the June and November series.

The pathway suits families planning university outside Zimbabwe, or who want the coursework and practical assessment components that IGCSE carries. It is not a premium tier — it costs the same as ZIMSEC, except for the external entry fees, which are set by Cambridge and passed on at cost.

The ZIMSEC pathway leads to Ordinary Level at the end of Form 4 and Advanced Level in the Upper Sixth. It remains the direct route to the University of Zimbabwe, NUST, MSU and the country's polytechnics, and it is the right choice for the majority of our pupils.

Forms 1 and 2 — everyone does everything

For two years there are no options. Every pupil takes fourteen subjects, including both a science practical rotation and a full arts rotation, so that the choice made at the end of Form 2 is informed rather than inherited. Setting happens in Mathematics and languages only, and it is reviewed each term.

Forms 3 and 4 — four option blocks

Pupils carry English, Mathematics, Combined or separate Sciences and one language, then choose four more from the blocks below. Both examination boards run through the same blocks, so a pathway change does not cost a subject.

How the menu is made

A four-week rotation set with a dietitian, published every Friday for the week ahead. Maize meal, vegetables and eggs come from the school farm; meat and dairy from suppliers within the province.

Allergies and diets

Every dish is marked V (vegetarian), H (halaal), GF (gluten free) or N (contains nuts). Pupils with declared allergies are flagged at the serving line and a named alternative is prepared, not improvised.

Feedback that changes things

A pupil food committee with two representatives per house meets the catering manager fortnightly. Parents can see the menu and rate meals in the portal; anything below three stars twice is dropped from the rotation.

Mental health is timetabled, not improvised

Two full-time counsellors hold open hours every day and pupils may self-refer without going through a teacher. Why we are teaching both Cambridge and ZIMSEC

Dr. Tendai Mukombachoto, Head of College

The question we are asked most often by prospective parents is which curriculum the school is really about. The assumption behind it is fair: most schools that offer two are, in practice, offering one properly.

I have taught in schools where the international stream got the new laboratory and the local stream got the timetable leftovers. I have also seen the reverse — an international programme bolted on for marketing, taught by whoever had a free period, quietly withering after three years. Both outcomes come from the same mistake: treating the pathway as a product tier rather than as an academic route.

Our position is that the examination a pupil sits should follow their plan. A child who will read medicine at the University of Zimbabwe is better served by ZIMSEC A Level, which the admissions office knows intimately and which is taught by teachers who have marked it. A child whose family is in Johannesburg or Birmingham is better served by Cambridge, whose grades need no translation. Neither of these is a judgement about the child.

What resourcing both actually requires

Three things, and they are expensive. First, teachers qualified on both syllabuses, which means recruiting people who have examined for Cambridge and people who have marked for ZIMSEC, and paying for the training that keeps both current. Second, a timetable built so that the option blocks are identical, which constrains everything else we can do with the week. Third, laboratory provision for the full practical requirement of both — Cambridge's coursework components and ZIMSEC's practical papers do not overlap as neatly as the brochures suggest.

We have budgeted for all three from the founding year rather than promising them for later. That is why the science block has eight laboratories for 420 pupils, which looks extravagant until you count the practicals both boards require in a single week of Term 2.

The part we will be judged on

Our first O Level and IGCSE cohort sits in November 2030. When those results arrive we will publish them in full — both boards, subject by subject, with the grade distribution and the value added against entry data. If one pathway has been quietly under-served, the numbers will say so, and we would rather you heard it from us.

Until then, all we can offer is the structure and the reasoning. Both are on this website, and I am happy to argue about either at an open day.
